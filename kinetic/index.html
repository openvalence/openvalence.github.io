Kinetic

The motion planner. It turns timed knots into jerk-limited motion, with bit-identical results on every chip.

A header-only C++ library with no hardware dependencies. It is the planner in Nucleus, and you can use it on its own. No release yet. Apache-2.0.

How it plans

  • Knots in, a sampled trajectory out

    Every source becomes knots: the curve passes position p at time t_us, optionally with an end velocity. The planner never sees a wire format, so a script and a live stream feed it the same way.

  • Event-driven

    Pending knots are solved when they change, at the next sample. The curve is continuous in position and velocity, so the motor never gets a step in speed.

  • Timing comes first

    A knot the limits cannot reach in time is moved toward the previous knot. It is never dropped or placed late, so a script keeps its timing and gives up some stroke length instead.

  • Limits are ceilings

    The planner stays under the velocity, acceleration and jerk limits and does not plan to reach them. Every trim, clamp and refusal is recorded as an anomaly you can read, so you can see where a pattern asked for too much.

Determinism

The plan you preview in the browser is the plan the firmware runs, as long as both are built with the same compiler flag.

Difference between native, WebAssembly and ESP32-P4 builds of the same plan, with -ffp-contract=off.
0ULP
How that holds

Quantities are float; time is uint64_t microseconds that you supply. The same calls give bit-identical results on every IEEE-754 target, as long as everything that compiles the planner uses -ffp-contract=off and not -ffast-math. Without the flag, GCC fuses multiply-adds on targets such as the ESP32-P4 and the results drift by a few ULPs from native and WebAssembly builds. The test suite pins a fingerprint of the rendered motion.

Using the library

You call submit() with knots and positionAt() on your own clock. It needs C++20 and builds with CMake or PlatformIO. A WebAssembly build with a small C interface is in the repo.

Units, and what it leaves to you

Position is normalized 0 to 1 across the stroke window you give it, so mapping to millimeters, steps or encoder counts is your code's job.

Kinetic plans motion inside a window. Homing, rail limits, e-stop, arbitration and step generation belong to the machine around it, and in OpenValence that is Nucleus.

In Nucleus, Valence motion segments become knots in the firmware's motion arbiter. The diagrams show where the planner sits, and Components lists the other parts.
